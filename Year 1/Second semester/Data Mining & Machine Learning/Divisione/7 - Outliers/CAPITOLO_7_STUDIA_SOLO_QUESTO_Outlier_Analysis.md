## 1. Outlier and Outlier Analysis

### What Are Outliers?

**Outlier**: a data object that **deviates significantly from the normal objects, as if it were generated by a DIFFERENT MECHANISM**.

*Examples*: an unusual credit card purchase; in sports, Michael Jordan, Wayne Gretzky.

### ⚠️ Outliers vs. Noise — and the tension between them

- **Noise** is **random error or variance** in a measured variable.
- **Noise should be REMOVED before outlier detection.**

> **But this creates a real challenge** (from the lecture): all noise-removal techniques are based on a **smoothing effect** — we have some variability in our signal and we smooth it to reduce that variability. **If we apply smoothing, we can reduce the amplitude of some samples, and this can PREVENT the identification of outliers.**
>
> **So: outliers are different from noise, but when we remove noise we should PRESERVE the outliers. We have to find a balance.**

**Outliers are interesting**: they **violate the mechanism that generates the normal data**.

### Outlier detection vs. novelty detection

> **The distinction** (lecture): a **novelty** is a new behaviour, different from the standard — its definition is similar to an outlier's. **The difference is that an OUTLIER REMAINS an outlier, while a NOVELTY is the starting point of a new type of object** — it is an outlier **only at the beginning**, and is **later merged into the model**.

**Applications**: credit card fraud detection, telecom fraud detection, customer segmentation, medical analysis.

> **Industrial motivation** (lecture): in industry, outlier detection is used as **fault detection** — important for understanding when a piece of equipment is heading toward a fault. **Of course we would like to PREDICT and not only DETECT faults.** Nowadays we have sensors everywhere, so collecting data is easy, and industry is very interested in using this data both to detect outliers and to predict possible faults.

### 1.1 Three Types of Outliers

#### (I) Global Outlier (point anomaly) — the most intuitive
- An object is a **global outlier $O_g$** if it **significantly deviates from the rest of the data set**.
- *Ex.*: intrusion detection in computer networks, fault detection in industry.
- **Issue: find an appropriate MEASUREMENT OF DEVIATION.**

> **The two sub-problems** (lecture): (1) we must be able to correctly **model the NORMAL behaviour** of the data, since detecting an outlier means detecting deviation from the rest; (2) we must understand what "**significantly**" means in practice — **how much** should an object deviate to be considered an outlier? We have to introduce **metrics and thresholds**.

#### (II) Contextual Outlier (conditional outlier)
- An object is a **contextual outlier $O_c$** if it **deviates significantly based on a SELECTED CONTEXT**.
- *Ex.*: **35 °C in Pisa — is it an outlier? It depends on whether it's summer or winter.**
- **Attributes of data objects must be divided into two groups:**
  - **Contextual attributes**: define the **context**, e.g., **time & location**.
  - **Behavioral attributes**: **characteristics of the object**, used in outlier evaluation, e.g., **temperature**.
- Can be viewed as a **generalization of LOCAL outliers** — whose density significantly deviates from its local area.
- **Issue: how to define or formulate a meaningful context?**

> **Why this is harder than the global case** (lecture): the problem is more complex because we must model **two different things — the context AND the behaviour**. And crucially, **most of the time we do NOT have this separation given to us**: we just have a set of attributes and **we must decide which are contextual and which are behavioral**. It's difficult to know which is which.
>
> **However, once the context is defined, we can apply the techniques for GLOBAL outliers.**

#### (III) Collective Outliers — the most complex situation
- **A SUBSET of data objects COLLECTIVELY deviates significantly from the whole data set, EVEN IF the individual data objects may NOT be outliers.**
- *Applications*: intrusion detection — e.g., **when a number of computers keep sending denial-of-service packages to each other**.
- Detection must consider **not only the behavior of individual objects, but also that of GROUPS of objects**.
- **Need background knowledge on the RELATIONSHIP among data objects**, such as a distance or similarity measure.

> **Why the usual techniques fail here** (lecture): many outlier-detection techniques are based on clustering algorithms. Recall **DBSCAN** — it generates clusters of different shapes and, implicitly, **also detects outliers**. But it detects **SINGLE** outliers. **When we want to detect a small GROUP of outliers, these clustering-based techniques cannot be used** — hence the need for specific techniques for collective outliers.

**⚠️ A data set may have multiple types of outliers, and ONE OBJECT MAY BELONG TO MORE THAN ONE TYPE.** *(Normally we focus on global outliers, in particular for industrial applications.)*

### 1.2 Challenges of Outlier Detection

- **Modeling normal objects and outliers properly**
  - Hard to **enumerate all possible normal behaviors** in an application.
  - **The border between normal and outlier objects is often a GRAY AREA.**
  > In all the techniques we'll see, we always have to select **parameters** on which outliers are identified — **if we change their values, the detected outliers change too.** Selecting them correctly depends on the application domain.
- **Application-specific outlier detection**
  - The choice of **distance measure** among objects and the model of relationships are often **application-dependent**.
  - *E.g.*, **clinical data: a SMALL deviation could be an outlier; in marketing analysis, we need very LARGE fluctuations** to decide for outliers.
- **Handling noise in outlier detection**
  - Noise may **distort the normal objects and blur the distinction** between normal objects and outliers. It **may help HIDE outliers** and reduce the effectiveness of detection.
- **Understandability** — *very important nowadays*
  - **Understand WHY these are outliers**: justification of the detection.
  - **Specify the DEGREE of an outlier**: the unlikelihood of the object being generated by a normal mechanism.
  > **The industrial perspective** (lecture): when discussing fault detection with companies, they want systems with good accuracy **but they are also very interested in understanding WHY they have these faults — the ROOT CAUSE — so they can correct the problem.** This is why understandability matters.

## 2. Outlier Detection Methods — the two categorizations

**Two ways to categorize outlier detection methods:**
1. **Based on whether user-labeled examples of outliers can be obtained**: **supervised, semi-supervised, vs. unsupervised** methods.
2. **Based on assumptions about normal data and outliers**: **statistical, proximity-based, and clustering-based** methods.

### 2.1 Supervised Methods

**Modeling outlier detection as a CLASSIFICATION problem.** We assume a labeled dataset with normal objects and outlier objects, and approach the problem as classical classification. Samples examined by **domain experts** are used for training & testing.

**Methods for learning a classifier for outlier detection effectively:**
- **Model normal objects** and report those **not matching** the model as outliers, **or**
- **Model outliers** and treat those not matching the model as normal.

**Challenges:**
- **⚠️ Imbalanced classes** — outliers are rare. Remedy: **boost the outlier class and make up some artificial outliers.** *(This is the big problem with these approaches: the number of outliers is very small relative to the total, so we have a heavily unbalanced dataset — see Chapter 4 §10.5.)*
- **Catch as many outliers as possible** — i.e., **RECALL is more important than accuracy** (rather than avoiding mislabeling normal objects as outliers).

### 2.2 Unsupervised Methods

- **Assume the normal objects are somewhat "clustered" into multiple groups**, each having some distinct features; we try to identify objects **not in any cluster**.
- **An outlier is expected to be FAR AWAY from any group of normal objects.**
- **⚠️ Weakness: CANNOT detect collective outliers effectively.** Normal objects may not share any strong patterns, but **the collective outliers may share HIGH SIMILARITY in a small area** — so they look like a legitimate little cluster.
- *Ex.*: in some intrusion or virus detection, **normal activities are diverse** → unsupervised methods may have a **high false positive rate** but still **miss many real outliers**. Supervised methods can be more effective, e.g., identifying attacks on some key resources.
- **Many clustering methods can be adapted**: find clusters, then outliers = those not belonging to any cluster.
  - **Problem 1: hard to distinguish NOISE from OUTLIERS.**
  - **Problem 2: costly**, since clustering comes first — but there are far fewer outliers than normal objects.
  - **Newer methods: tackle outliers DIRECTLY.**

### 2.3 Semi-Supervised Methods

- **Situation**: in many applications the number of labeled data is small. **Labels could be on outliers only, normal objects only, or both.**
- **If some labeled NORMAL objects are available**: use the labeled examples **and the proximate unlabeled objects** to train a model for normal objects; those **not fitting** the model are detected as outliers.
- **If only some labeled OUTLIERS are available**: a small number of labeled outliers may **not cover the possible outliers well** → to improve quality, **get help from models for normal objects learned by UNSUPERVISED methods.**

### 2.4 The three technique families (preview)

| Family | Assumption | Example figure reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| **Statistical (model-based)** | The **normal data follow some statistical model** (a stochastic model). **Data not following the model are outliers.** | First use a **Gaussian** to model the normal data. For each object $y$ in region $R$, estimate $g_D(y)$, the probability that $y$ fits the Gaussian. **If $g_D(y)$ is very low, $y$ is unlikely generated by the model → outlier.** |
| **Proximity-based** | An object is an outlier if **the nearest neighbors of the object are far away** — i.e., the **proximity** of the object **significantly deviates** from the proximity of most other objects. | Model the proximity of an object using its **3 nearest neighbors**. Objects in region $R$ are substantially different → outliers. |
| **Clustering-based** | **Normal data belong to LARGE and DENSE clusters**, whereas outliers belong to **small or sparse clusters, or to no cluster at all**. | Two clusters: all points not in $R$ form a large cluster; the two points in $R$ form a **tiny** cluster → outliers. |

**Notes:**
- **Statistical**: effectiveness **highly depends on whether the assumption of the statistical model holds** in the real data. Rich alternatives exist (parametric vs. non-parametric).
- **Proximity-based**: effectiveness **highly relies on the proximity measure**; in some applications proximity/distance measures **cannot be obtained easily**; and they **often have difficulty finding a GROUP of outliers that stay close to each other**. Two major types: **distance-based vs. density-based**.
- **Clustering-based**: since there are many clustering methods, there are many clustering-based detection methods. **Clustering is expensive** — a straightforward adaptation can be costly and **does not scale up well** for large data sets.

## 3. Statistical Approaches

**Assumption**: the objects in a data set are generated by a **stochastic process (a generative model)**.

**Idea: LEARN a generative model fitting the given data set, then identify the objects in LOW PROBABILITY REGIONS of the model as outliers.**
> *E.g.*, if we model our objects as a Gaussian distribution, points very far from the mean are considered outliers.

**Two categories:**

| | **Parametric** | **Non-parametric** |
|---|---|---|
| Assumption | Normal data is generated by a **parametric distribution with parameter $\theta$**. The distribution is **assumed**; we find the parameter. | **Do NOT assume an a-priori statistical model** — determine the model from the input data. |
| Detection | The **probability density function** $f(x,\theta)$ gives the probability that object $x$ is generated by the distribution. **The smaller this value, the more likely $x$ is an outlier.** | Model learned from data; low estimated density → outlier. |
| Parameters | E.g., for a Gaussian: identify **mean and standard deviation**. | **⚠️ Not completely parameter-free** — the *number and nature* of parameters are **flexible and not fixed in advance**. *(E.g., for a histogram you still must set the bin width.)* |
| Examples | Gaussian, mixture models | **Histogram**, **kernel density estimation** |

### 3.1 Parametric Methods I — Univariate outliers under a Normal distribution

**Univariate data**: a data set involving **only one attribute or variable**.

We often assume data are generated from a **normal distribution**, learn the parameters ($\mu$, $\sigma$) from the input data, and identify the points with **low probability** as outliers.

> **⚠️ But FIRST we must ask: is the distribution actually normal?** We need to verify the assumption with a test:
> - **Shapiro-Wilk Test**: tests whether a random sample comes from a normal distribution.
> - **Q-Q (Quantile-Quantile) plot**: a **visual** approach — can estimate whether a random sample comes from a continuous distribution (e.g., normal), as long as the quantiles can be calculated.

#### The Q-Q plot procedure

**Q-Q plot** = plots of **two quantiles against each other**. *(Recall: a quantile is a fraction where certain values fall below that quantile.)* To verify normality, **plot the quantiles corresponding to the random samples against the quantiles of a normal distribution**: on the x-axis the synthetic normal data, on the y-axis the data being analyzed.

> **If the Q-Q plot is close to a STRAIGHT LINE, the distribution is approximately normal. If it is far from a line, we cannot conclude normality.**

**Worked example** — determine whether these samples come from a normal distribution:
`7.19, 6.31, 5.89, 4.5, 3.77, 4.25, 5.19, 5.79, 6.79`

**Step 1. Sort the values** from smallest to largest:
`3.77, 4.25, 4.50, 5.19, 5.79, 5.89, 6.31, 6.79, 7.19`

**Step 2. Draw a normal distribution curve and divide it into $n+1$ segments of EQUAL AREA**, where $n$ is the number of values. Here $n = 9$ values → **10 regions**, each of **10% of the total area**.

**Step 3. Find the z-value** (cut-off point for each segment). *Recall: the z-value is the number of standard deviations from the mean a data point is.*

| Cumulative % | z-value | | Cumulative % | z-value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10% | −1.28 | | 60% | 0.25 |
| 20% | −0.84 | | 70% | 0.52 |
| 30% | −0.52 | | 80% | 0.84 |
| 40% | −0.25 | | 90% | 1.28 |
| 50% | 0 | | 100% | 3.0 |

> **The z-score identity worth internalizing**: $z = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$, therefore $\sigma\cdot z = x - \mu$. **If $z = 2$, it means $x$ is 2 standard deviations away from the mean.**

**Step 4. Plot the data set values against the normal distribution cut-off points.** **An (almost) straight line indicates the data distribution is approximately normal.**

#### Method 1 — Maximum likelihood estimation of the Gaussian

Model the normal distribution and consider the probability of the points belonging to it. Normal data will belong with **high probability**; outliers will have a **low probability** value.

*Ex.* Average temperatures: `{24.0, 28.9, 28.9, 29.0, 29.1, 29.1, 29.2, 29.2, 29.3, 29.4}`

Use the **maximum likelihood method** to estimate $\mu$ and $\sigma$. The probability that a point $x_i$ is generated by the model is:

$$P(x_i) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}e^{-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$

Consequently, the **likelihood** that the whole sample $X$ is generated by the model is:

$$L(\mu,\sigma \mid X) = \prod_{i=1}^{n}P(x_i) = \prod_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma}e^{-\frac{(x_i-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}}$$

The task is to find the parameters **maximizing the likelihood**. **Taking derivatives with respect to $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$**, we obtain the maximum likelihood estimates — which turn out to be simply the sample mean and sample standard deviation:

$$\hat{\mu} = \bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_i, \qquad \hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i - \bar{x})^2$$

**For the above data with $n=10$**: $\hat\mu = 28.61$, $\hat\sigma = 1.51$.

**Applying the 3-sigma rule** (recall from Chapter 2: the $\mu \pm 3\sigma$ region contains **99.7%** of the data):

$$\frac{24 - 28.61}{1.51} = -3.04 < -3 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \textbf{24 is an OUTLIER}$$

> **Summary of the method**: (1) check the distribution is normal with a test; (2) compute $\mu$ and $\sigma$; (3) check the probability of each data point on that distribution to conclude whether it's an outlier.
>
> **⚠️ What are the PARAMETERS in this approach?** $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are **not** parameters — they are **computed** from the data. **The parameter is the THRESHOLD**: we must decide whether to use $\mu \pm 3\sigma$ or some other value.

#### Method 2 — The Grubbs' Test (maximum normed residual test)

Another statistical method under the normal distribution. **The test finds whether a MINIMUM value or a MAXIMUM value is an outlier.**
> *Why min or max*: since we're in a **univariate** scenario with just one variable, an outlier can only be a minimum or a maximum value.

The test checks for outliers by looking for **the maximum of the absolute differences between the values and the mean**.

**Steps:**
1. **Find the G test statistic.**
2. **Find the G critical value** (from tables or software libraries).
3. **Compare the test statistic to the G critical value.**
4. **Reject the point as an outlier if the test statistic is GREATER than the critical value.**

**1. Finding the G test statistic:**
- a) Order the data points from smallest to largest.
- b) Find the mean $\bar{Y}$ and standard deviation $s$ of the data set.
- c) Calculate:

$$G = \frac{\max_{i}\big|Y_i - \bar{Y}\big|}{s}$$

**2. Finding the G critical value**: several tables exist, giving critical values for various $N$ and $\alpha$ levels.
> **On $\alpha$**: it is the significance level — **the lower the $\alpha$, the higher the confidence** in the decision.

Manually it can be computed as:

$$G > \frac{N-1}{\sqrt{N}}\sqrt{\frac{t^2_{\alpha/(2N),\,N-2}}{N-2+t^2_{\alpha/(2N),\,N-2}}}$$

where $t_{\alpha/(2N),\,N-2}$ is the value taken by a **t-distribution with $(N-2)$ degrees of freedom** at a significance level of $\alpha/(2N)$.

**3. Compare:**
- **$G_{test} < G_{critical}$** → keep the sample; **it is NOT an outlier.**
- **$G_{test} \ge G_{critical}$** → **reject the sample as an OUTLIER.**

**Worked example:**

Data: `145, 125, 190, 135, 220, 130, 210, 3, 165, 165, 150`

| | |
|---|---|
| min | **3** |
| mean | 148.9091 |
| stdev | 57.81082 |
| **G** | **2.523906** |
| alpha | 0.05 |
| N | 11 |

From the table, $G_{crit} = 2.23$. **Thus $G > G_{crit}$** → **the minimum value, 3, is really an OUTLIER.**

### 3.2 Parametric Methods II — Multivariate Outliers

**Multivariate data**: a data set involving **two or more** attributes.

> **The philosophy: TRANSFORM the multivariate outlier detection task into a UNIVARIATE outlier detection problem**, so we can apply the methods described above. The standard way is to **compute the DISTANCE between objects** — instead of directly evaluating whether an object is an outlier, we compute the distance between one object and the others, turning the problem univariate **because the distance is just a single value**.

#### Method 1 — The Mahalanobis distance

**Mahalanobis' distance is the distance between a POINT and a DISTRIBUTION** — not between two distinct points. It is effectively **the multivariate equivalent of the Euclidean distance**.

**Why the Euclidean distance is insufficient** — it works fine **only as long as the dimensions are (a) equally weighted and (b) independent of each other**:

**(a) Equally weighted.** Two tables showing 'area' and 'price' of the same objects, where **only the UNITS of the variables change**, nevertheless produce **different distances between any two rows**.
> **Remedy**: **scale the variables** — compute the **z-score** $(x-\text{mean})/\text{std}$, or make them vary within a specific range (e.g. between 0 and 1). *(See Chapter 3 §5.1.)*

**(b) Independent of each other.** The Euclidean distance between a point and the center of the distribution **can give little or misleading information about how close a point really is to the cluster**.

> **The worked intuition** (lecture): consider two scatter plots.
> - **Left**: points equally distributed in the plane → **no correlation** between the two attributes. Applying the Euclidean distance, the distance from the center to point 1 and to point 2 is similar — and this **looks correct**.
> - **Right**: X and Y are **correlated** — the points lie along a line. **The distance from the black point to point 2 should be considered HIGHER than the distance to point 1**, because **point 1 lies along the same distribution/cluster**. **The Euclidean distance cannot capture correlation, because it has no weights — it treats all features the same way.**

**How Mahalanobis differs from Euclidean — three steps:**
1. **Transforms the columns into UNCORRELATED variables**
2. **Scales the columns to make their variance equal to 1**
3. **Finally, it calculates the Euclidean distance**

> **It applies a sort of transformation — like a ROTATION OF THE AXES**, similar to what we saw with **Principal Component Analysis** (Chapter 3 §4.1). After the transformation, we can apply the Euclidean distance.

Let $\bar{o}$ be the **mean vector** for a multivariate data set. The Mahalanobis distance for an object $o$ to $\bar{o}$ is:

$$MDist^2(o,\bar{o}) = (o-\bar{o})^{T}\,S^{-1}\,(o-\bar{o})$$

where **$S$ is the COVARIANCE MATRIX**.

> **Dividing by the covariance matrix is essentially the multivariate equivalent of the regular standardization $z=(x-\text{mean})/\text{std}$.**
>
> **⚠️ The key behaviour: if the variables are strongly CORRELATED, the covariance will be HIGH and the distance will be REDUCED; otherwise the covariance will be LOW and the distance will NOT be reduced.**

**The procedure:**
1. **Calculate the mean vector** $\bar{o}$ from the multivariate data set.
2. **For each object $o$, calculate $MDist(o,\bar{o})$** — the Mahalanobis distance from $o$ to $\bar{o}$.
3. **Detect outliers in the TRANSFORMED UNIVARIATE data set** $\{MDist(o,\bar{o}) \mid o \in D\}$ — **e.g. using Grubbs' test.**
4. **If $MDist(o,\bar{o})$ is determined to be an outlier, then $o$ is regarded as an outlier as well.**

> **Step 2 is the transformation step**: we associate with each object a **single real value** (its distance to the mean object), so after step 2 we simply have a set of values and can apply the univariate techniques.

**Weaknesses:**
- **Computationally heavy** (covariance matrix and its **inverse**).
- **Needs to store** the covariance matrix and its inverse.

#### Method 2 — The $\chi^2$-statistic

- **Assumption**: the population of $O$ follows a **multivariate distribution** with mean vector $\bar{o}$ and covariance matrix $S$.
- The method exploits a distance measure based on the **chi-square test statistic**:

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(o_i - E_i)^2}{E_i}$$

where $o_i$ and $E_i$ are the **observed** and **expected** values of the $i$-th variable, and $n$ is the **number of variables**. **Using the average values as estimates of the expectation.**
- **According to the CENTRAL LIMIT THEOREM**, when the number of variables is **large enough (i.e., greater than 30)**, $\chi^2$ — as the sum of squared differences between observed and expected values — has **approximately a NORMAL distribution**.
- Since we are interested in detecting **significantly large $\chi^2$ values**, we need to set only the **UPPER control limit** $\overline{\chi^2} + 3S_{\chi^2}$: **if the computed $\chi^2$ for an observation exceeds it, we signal an anomaly.**

### 3.3 Non-Parametric Methods

**The model of normal data is learned from the input data WITHOUT any a-priori structure.** These often make **fewer assumptions** about the data and are thus **applicable in more scenarios**.

#### Detection using a Histogram

> The figure shows a histogram of purchase amounts in transactions. *(Recall from Chapter 2: the **area** of the bar indicates the frequency.)* **A transaction in the amount of \$7,500 is an outlier, since only 0.2% of transactions have an amount higher than \$5,000.**
>
> So: plot the histogram, **fix a threshold on the probability**, and decide that values above the threshold are outliers.

**⚠️ Problem: hard to choose an appropriate BIN SIZE.**
- **Too SMALL a bin size** → normal objects fall in **empty/rare bins** → **FALSE POSITIVES**.
- **Too BIG a bin size** → outliers get hidden **in some frequent bins** → **FALSE NEGATIVES**.

**Solution: adopt kernel density estimation** to estimate the probability density distribution of the data. **If the estimated density function is high, the object is likely normal; otherwise it is likely an outlier.**

#### Kernel Density Estimation

> **The approach is analogous to DENCLUE** (Chapter 5 §5.6): we place a kernel function on each value and define a **global function** that is the **sum** of these kernel functions. **Normal behavior is characterized by a HIGH value of the global function; outliers by the opposite.** This also lets us see what thresholds are reasonable, just by observing the global density estimate.

$$\hat{f}_h(x) = \frac{1}{nh}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\!\left(\frac{x-x_i}{h}\right)$$

where **$K(\cdot)$ is the kernel** — a **symmetric** but not necessarily positive function that **integrates to one** — and **$h>0$ is a smoothing parameter called the BANDWIDTH**.

> **⚠️ Are there parameters here?** **YES — the BANDWIDTH $h$**, which for a standard (Gaussian) kernel is the **standard deviation**. So even with "non-parametric" approaches, **we still have parameters to set.**

### 3.4 Statistical Methods: Computational Cost

**The computational cost depends on the models:**
- **Simple parametric models** (e.g., a Gaussian) → **LINEAR time**.
- **More sophisticated models** (e.g., **mixture models**, where the **Expectation Maximization (EM)** algorithm is used in learning) → **several iterations**; each iteration, however, is typically **linear** in the data set's size.
- **For kernel density estimation, the model learning cost can be up to QUADRATIC.**
- **⚠️ Once the model is learned, the outlier detection cost is often VERY SMALL per object.**

## 4. Proximity-Based Approaches

**Intuition**: objects that are **far away from the others** are outliers.

**Assumption**: **the PROXIMITY of an outlier deviates significantly from that of most of the others in the data set.**

> **Two things must be defined** (lecture): (1) **how we compute the DISTANCE**, and (2) **what counts as a "significant" deviation** — again we must introduce a **threshold**.

**Two types of proximity-based outlier detection methods:**
- **Distance-based**: an object $o$ is an outlier if its **neighborhood does not have ENOUGH other points**.
- **Density-based**: an object $o$ is an outlier if its **density is relatively much LOWER than that of its neighbors**.

### 4.1 Distance-Based Outlier Detection — the DB(r, π) outlier

For each object $o$, examine the **number of other objects in the $r$-neighborhood** of $o$, where **$r$ is a user-specified distance threshold**. An object $o$ is an outlier if **most** of the objects in $D$ (taking **$\pi$ as a fraction threshold**) are **far away** from $o$ — i.e., **not in the $r$-neighborhood of $o$**.

**An object $o$ is a DB($r$, $\pi$) outlier if:**

$$\frac{\big\|\{o' \mid dist(o,o')\le r\}\big\|}{\|D\|} \le \pi$$

**Equivalently**, one can check the distance between $o$ and its **$k$-th nearest neighbor $o_k$**, where $k = \lceil \pi\cdot\|D\| \rceil$: **$o$ is an outlier if $dist(o, o_k) > r$.**

> **The two equivalent formulations, unpacked:**
> - **First form**: take $o$, draw a **sphere of radius $r$**, and count how many other objects fall inside. Our threshold is $\pi$, but $\pi$ is a **percentage** of the total number of instances $\|D\|$ — so the threshold **as an object count** is $\pi\cdot\|D\|$. If the count $\le \pi\cdot\|D\|$ → outlier.
> - **Second form**: compute the distance from $o$ to all other elements, **sort them from nearest to farthest, and take the $k$-th** — where $k$ is exactly the threshold rank $\pi\cdot\|D\|$. If that distance is $< r$, it is **not** an outlier (there are at least $\pi\cdot\|D\|$ points inside the sphere); otherwise it **is**.
> - **Numerically**: with $\|D\|=1000$ and $\pi=10\%$, the threshold count is **100**. If the sphere of radius $r$ contains at least 100 elements → fine; or equivalently, if the **100th nearest** element is at distance $< r$ → fine.

#### ⚠️ The conceptual limitation of DB(r, π)

**First**, we must set **two parameters, $r$ and $\pi$** — not easy, since we know little about the dataset's distribution but we want to describe outliers.

**Second — and this is the fundamental problem:**
> **If the whole space is uniformly sparse or uniformly dense, that's fine** — a sparse space just needs large $r$ and small $\pi$. **The problem arises when BOTH cases occur in the same dataset.** If we tune $r$ and $\pi$ for the **dense** zone (small $r$, high $\pi$), then when we reach the **sparse** zone **all the points there will be flagged as outliers**.
>
> **We set two parameters for the OVERALL space, but with different densities in different regions these two parameters cannot work properly everywhere. THIS IS A CONCEPTUAL PROBLEM, NOT AN OPTIMIZATION PROBLEM — we cannot find a solution within this framework.**

#### Computational cost and the Nested Loop optimization

To evaluate whether a **single** object $o$ is an outlier, we must compute the distance between it and **all** other objects → **$O(n)$ per object, $O(n^2)$ overall**.

**Nested-loop optimization:** for any object $o_i$, calculate its distance from other objects and count the number in the $r$-neighborhood. **If $\pi\cdot n$ other objects are found within distance $r$, TERMINATE the inner loop** (it cannot be an outlier). Otherwise, $o_i$ **is** a DB($r$, $\pi$) outlier.

> **Efficiency: actual CPU time is NOT $O(n^2)$ but LINEAR in the data set size, since for most non-outlier objects the inner loop terminates EARLY.**

```
Algorithm: Distance-based outlier detection.
Input:  a set of objects D = {o_1, ..., o_n}; thresholds r (r > 0) and π (0 < π ≤ 1)
Output: DB(r, π) outliers in D

for i = 1 to n do
    count = 0
    for j = 1 to n do
        if i ≠ j and dist(o_i, o_j) ≤ r then
            count = count + 1
            if count ≥ π · n then
                exit                      {o_i cannot be a DB(r, π) outlier}
            endif
        endif
    endfor
    print o_i                             {o_i is a DB(r, π) outlier}
endfor
```

### 4.2 A Grid-Based Method (CELL)

**Why efficiency is still a concern**: when the complete set of objects **cannot be held in main memory**, we pay **I/O swapping** costs. **The major costs**: (1) each object is tested against the **whole data set** — why not only its close neighbours? (2) objects are checked **one by one** — why not **group by group**?

**Grid-based method (CELL)**: the data space is partitioned into a **multi-dimensional grid**. **Each cell is a hypercube with DIAGONAL LENGTH $r/2$.**

> **The choice of $r/2$ is not by chance** — it lets us make preliminary decisions about which objects can or cannot be outliers.

**Two levels of cells:**
- **Level-1**: for any possible point $x$ in cell $C$ and any possible point $y$ in a **level-1 cell** (one cell away from $C$): **$dist(x,y) \le r$**.
- **Level-2**: for any possible point $x$ in cell $C$ and any point $y$ in a **level-2 cell** (two cells away from $C$): **$dist(x,y) \ge r$**.

> **Why the diagonal $r/2$ gives exactly this**: take a cell $C$ and its level-1 neighbour. Two points, one in each, even placed at the two most extreme opposite corners, are at distance at most $r$ — because **the sum of the two diagonals is $r/2 + r/2 = r$**. For a **level-2** cell (two cells away), the distance is necessarily $\ge r$.

**The pruning rules.** Let **$a$** = number of objects in cell $C$, **$b_1$** = total objects in the level-1 cells, **$b_2$** = total objects in the level-2 cells:

- **Level-1 cell pruning rule**: if $a + b_1 > \lceil\pi n\rceil$, then **EVERY object $o$ in $C$ is NOT a DB($r$,$\pi$)-outlier** — because all those objects in $C$ and the level-1 cells are in the $r$-neighborhood of $o$, and there are at least $\lceil\pi n\rceil$ such neighbors.
- **Level-2 cell pruning rule**: if $a + b_1 + b_2 < \lceil\pi n\rceil + 1$, then **ALL objects in $C$ ARE DB($r$,$\pi$)-outliers** — because each of their $r$-neighborhoods has fewer than $\lceil\pi n\rceil$ other objects.

> **⚠️ Both rules hold in ONE DIRECTION ONLY.** If the level-1 condition is **not** satisfied, we **cannot** conclude that the objects in $C$ are outliers — they might be, but we must verify. This is because a point in a corner of $C$, with radius $r$, also reaches some **level-2** elements that this (very strict) condition ignores. The rules serve only to **remove objects from further evaluation**.

**Thus we only need to check the objects that CANNOT be pruned — and even for such an object $o$, we only need to compute the distance between $o$ and the objects in the LEVEL-2 cells** (since beyond level-2, the distance from $o$ is more than $r$).

### 4.3 Local Distance-Based Outlier Detection — Outlierness

**The problem to solve**: different densities. In the standard figure, **outlier $o_2$ has a density similar to the elements of cluster $C_1$**.
> If we tune $r$ and $\pi$ for the dense zone $C_2$, **all the instances in $C_1$** become outliers (especially $o_2$). If we tune them for the sparse zone, **$o_2$ gets absorbed into $C_2$.**

**The solution — instead of the two global parameters $\pi$ and $r$, reason about the situation in the NEIGHBORHOOD:**

- Let **$N_k(x_i)$** be the **$k$-nearest neighbors** of $x_i$.
- Let **$D_k(x_i)$** be the **average distance to the $k$-nearest neighbors**:

$$D_k(x_i) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in N_k(x_i)}\lVert x_i - x_j\rVert$$

- **Outlierness** is the **ratio of $D_k(x_i)$ to the AVERAGE $D_k(x_j)$ of its neighbors $j$**:

$$O_k(x_i) = \frac{D_k(x_i)}{\dfrac{1}{k}\displaystyle\sum_{j\in N_k(x_i)}D_k(x_j)}$$

**If outlierness $> 1$, $x_i$ is FURTHER AWAY from its neighbors than expected.**

> **Why this solves the varying-density problem**: with outlierness **we do NOT fix our idea of "outlier" for the overall space — we ADAPT to the density we have locally.** We compare the average distance from the object to its neighbours against the average distance of **those neighbours to THEIR neighbours**. **It is like placing the object in the CONTEXT in which it sits.**
>
> **Concretely**: for a point inside $C_1$ with $k=3$, its average distance to its 3 neighbours is **comparable** to each of those neighbours' average distance to their own 3 neighbours → outlierness ≈ 1 → normal. For **$o_2$**, the average distance to its neighbours is **higher** than the average distance among those neighbours → outlierness > 1 → flagged, **because $o_2$ is a LOCAL outlier close to a high-density zone**.
>
> **Result**: unlike plain DB($r$,$\pi$) — which would only have found $o_1$ — **all elements of $C_1$ and $C_2$ are correctly non-outliers, and the only two outliers are $o_1$ and $o_2$.** But **the computational cost is high.**

#### ⚠️ Where outlierness FAILS

**If clusters with different densities are CLOSE, outlierness gives unintuitive results — the problem is along the BOUNDARIES.**

> In the standard example, **$p$ has higher outlierness than $q$ and $r$** — because **the green points are not part of the KNN list of $p$ for small $k$**. **But $p$ is NOT an outlier, while $q$ and $r$ are.** *(This is a very particular case.)*

#### Parameters of the outlierness approach

- **$k$** — obviously a parameter, and **the approach is VERY SENSITIVE to it**.
- **⚠️ A threshold** — because **outlierness does NOT itself decide whether $x_i$ is an outlier**; it is just a **value associated with $x_i$**, computed for all points. To decide, we need a threshold (we end up with a **univariate distribution** of outlierness values, so we can also apply the univariate methods of §3 to it).

### 4.4 Density-Based Outlier Detection — LOF

**Local outliers**: outliers **compared to their LOCAL neighborhoods**, instead of the global data distribution.

> In the standard figure: **$o_1$ and $o_2$ are LOCAL outliers to $C_1$; $o_3$ is a GLOBAL outlier; but $o_4$ is NOT an outlier.** However, **proximity-based clustering cannot find that $o_1$ and $o_2$ are outliers** (e.g., comparing with $o_4$).

**Intuition**: **the density around an outlier object is significantly different from the density around its neighbors.**
> For a point in $C_1$, the density around it is similar to the density around its neighbours — same for a point in $C_2$. **For $o_1$ or $o_2$, the density around is LOWER than the density around their neighbours.**

**Method: use the RELATIVE density of an object against its neighbors as the indicator of the degree of the object being an outlier.**

**Definitions:**
- **$k$-distance of an object $o$**, $dist_k(o)$: the distance between $o$ and its **$k$-th nearest neighbor**.
- **$k$-distance neighborhood of $o$**: $N_k(o) = \{o' \mid o'\in D,\ dist(o,o') \le dist_k(o)\}$
  > **⚠️ $N_k(o)$ could be BIGGER than $k$**, since multiple objects may have **identical** distance to $o$.

**Reachability distance from $o'$ to $o$:**

$$reachdist_k(o' \leftarrow o) = \max\{\,dist_k(o'),\ dist(o,o')\,\}$$

where $k$ is a user-specified parameter.
> **Meaning**: if the point is **inside** the neighbourhood, the distance is **clamped to $dist_k(o')$**.

**Local reachability density of $o$:**

$$lrd_k(o) = \frac{\lVert N_k(o)\rVert}{\displaystyle\sum_{o'\in N_k(o)} reachdist_k(o'\leftarrow o)}$$

> **This looks like a density** because we have a **count** at the numerator and a **sort of volume** at the denominator.

**LOF (Local Outlier Factor)** of an object $o$ — the **average of the RATIO of the local reachability density of $o$ and those of $o$'s $k$-nearest neighbors**:

$$LOF_k(o) = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{o'\in N_k(o)}\frac{lrd_k(o')}{lrd_k(o)}}{\lVert N_k(o)\rVert}$$

> **The lower the local reachability density of $o$, and the higher the local reachability density of the kNN of $o$, the HIGHER the LOF.** **This captures a local outlier whose local density is relatively low compared to the local densities of its kNN.**

**Interpretation:**
- If the density of $o$ is **similar** to that of its neighbours, $lrd_k(o')\approx lrd_k(o)$, so the sum is approximately $\lVert N_k(o)\rVert$ → **LOF ≈ 1 → NOT an outlier.**
- **If LOF is much higher than 1 → $o$ is probably an OUTLIER.**

> **⚠️ Does LOF solve the "close clusters of different density" problem?** **NO — using density we have the SAME problem in that case.**

**Parameters**: **$k$** (everything is defined in terms of it) and **a threshold** on LOF to decide.

#### Worked example — LOF step by step

Four data points: **a(0,0), b(0,1), c(1,1), d(3,0)**. Plotting them, we expect a **higher value for $d$**, which is far from the others.

**Step 1 — all pairwise distances (Manhattan distance):**

| pair | dist | | pair | dist |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| dist(a,b) | 1 | | dist(b,c) | 1 |
| dist(a,c) | 2 | | dist(b,d) | 4 |
| dist(a,d) | 3 | | dist(c,d) | 3 |

**Step 2 — fix $k = 2$, then compute $dist_2$ and $N_2$:**

| object | $dist_2$ | $N_2$ |
|---|---|---|
| a | $dist(a,c) = 2$ | {b, c} |
| b | $dist(b,a) = 1$ | {a, c} |
| c | $dist(c,a) = 2$ | {b, a} |
| d | $dist(d,a) = 3$ | {a, c} |

**Step 3 — local reachability densities.** For example, for $a$:
$$reachdist_2(b\leftarrow a) = \max\{dist_2(b),\ dist(b,a)\} = \max\{1,1\} = 1$$
$$reachdist_2(c\leftarrow a) = \max\{dist_2(c),\ dist(c,a)\} = \max\{2,2\} = 2$$
$$lrd_2(a) = \frac{\lVert N_2(a)\rVert}{reachdist_2(b\leftarrow a)+reachdist_2(c\leftarrow a)} = \frac{2}{1+2} = 0.667$$

Similarly:
$$lrd_2(b) = \frac{2}{reachdist_2(a\leftarrow b)+reachdist_2(c\leftarrow b)} = \frac{2}{2+2} = 0.5$$
$$lrd_2(c) = \frac{2}{reachdist_2(b\leftarrow c)+reachdist_2(a\leftarrow c)} = \frac{2}{1+2} = 0.667$$
$$lrd_2(d) = \frac{2}{reachdist_2(a\leftarrow d)+reachdist_2(c\leftarrow d)} = \frac{2}{3+3} = 0.33$$

**Step 4 — LOF.** The course materials compute LOF in the **expanded form**:

$$LOF_k(o) = \Big[\textstyle\sum_{o'\in N_k(o)}lrd_k(o')\Big]\times\Big[\textstyle\sum_{o'\in N_k(o)}reachdist_k(o'\leftarrow o)\Big]$$

$$LOF_2(a) = (lrd_2(b)+lrd_2(c))\cdot(reachdist_2(b\!\leftarrow\!a)+reachdist_2(c\!\leftarrow\!a)) = (0.5+0.667)(1+2) = 3.501$$
$$LOF_2(b) = (0.667+0.667)(2+2) = 5.336$$
$$LOF_2(c) = (0.5+0.667)(1+2) = 3.501$$
$$LOF_2(d) = (0.667+0.667)(3+3) = 8.004$$

**The sorted order:**
$$LOF_2(d) = 8.004 \ >\ LOF_2(b) = 5.336 \ >\ LOF_2(a) = LOF_2(c) = 3.501$$

**→ Obviously, the top-1 outlier is point $d$, as expected.**

> **⚠️ Worth knowing for the exam**: the expanded form above **omits the $1/\lVert N_k(o)\rVert^2$ normalization** present in the ratio-form definition. Since $\sum reachdist = \lVert N_k(o)\rVert / lrd_k(o)$, the two differ by a constant factor $\lVert N_k(o)\rVert^2$ (here $2^2=4$): the normalized values would be $d = 2.00$, $b = 1.33$, $a = c = 0.875$. **The RANKING — and therefore the answer "$d$ is the outlier" — is identical**, because all points here have the same $\lVert N_k\rVert$. But note that the "**LOF ≈ 1 means normal**" interpretation rule applies to the **normalized** form. **Reproduce whichever form the exercise uses; the ordering is what matters.**

## 5. Clustering-Based Approaches

**An object is an outlier if:**
1. **it does NOT belong to any cluster**, or
2. **there is a LARGE DISTANCE between the object and its closest cluster**, or
3. **it belongs to a SMALL or SPARSE cluster**.

> **The idea** (lecture): exploit clustering approaches, since **outlier detection is a SIDE EFFECT** of clustering. The techniques here are **not specific to outlier detection** — they are clustering techniques. We determine clusters and then analyze whether we have isolated points, or points inside a cluster that are very far from the others.

### Case 1 — Not belonging to any cluster

**Identify "animals not part of a flock"** using a **density-based clustering method such as DBSCAN** (Chapter 5 §5.2).

> DBSCAN follows the density of the points to build clusters; **where there is a low-density zone we get a separation between clusters.** What is interesting is that it can determine **clusters of different shapes** and gives the **side effect of identifying outliers** — in DBSCAN they are denoted as **noise**, but they are outliers.
>
> **In this first family, applying the clustering algorithm DIRECTLY gives us the outliers.**

### Case 2 — Far from its closest cluster

Using **k-means**, partition data points into clusters. For each object $o$, **assign an OUTLIER SCORE based on its distance from its closest center**:

$$\text{if } \frac{dist(o,\,c_o)}{avg\_dist(c_o)} \text{ is large} \quad\Rightarrow\quad o \text{ is likely an outlier}$$

> **Why extra work is needed here** (lecture): the peculiarity of k-means is that **at the end ALL objects are inside a cluster** — so it **cannot** directly provide outliers. We must work **after** applying k-means, analyzing whether **inside a cluster** there are objects deviating significantly from the others. Since each k-means cluster is represented by a **prototype (centroid)**, we can check for outliers by computing the **distance between the object and the centroid** of its cluster.

*Ex. Intrusion detection*: consider the similarity between data points and the clusters in a training data set.
- Use a training set to find patterns of "normal" data — e.g., frequent itemsets in each segment — and **cluster similar connections into groups**.
- **Compare new data points with the mined clusters — outliers are possible attacks** (TCP connection data example).

### Case 3 — Detecting Outliers in SMALL Clusters (CBLOF)

> **⚠️ Each of the approaches seen so far detects only INDIVIDUAL objects as outliers.** (Recall the proximity and statistical approaches — all detect single outliers.)
>
> **But in a large data set, some outliers may form a SMALL CLUSTER.** In intrusion detection, for example, **hackers use similar tactics to attack a system.** In the standard figure, **$C_1$ and $C_3$ should be regarded as outliers** — they are **COLLECTIVE outliers**: small groups of objects that significantly deviate from the remaining objects.

**CBLOF (Cluster-Based Local Outlier Factor)** is an outlier factor associated with each object. **It measures BOTH (a) the SIZE of the cluster the object belongs to AND (b) the DISTANCE between the object and its closest cluster.**

**The intuition: a point is anomalous if it belongs to a SMALL cluster, OR if it is FAR from the center of its cluster.**
> So with CBLOF we can detect **both** outliers that belong to a large cluster but sit very far from its center, **and** outliers that live in small clusters.

**Defining the large/small boundary.** Given two parameters $\alpha$ and $\beta$, sort the clusters by **decreasing cardinality** and define $b$ as the boundary between large and small clusters if **one of the following holds**:

$$\begin{cases} |C_1| + |C_2| + \dots + |C_b| \ \ge\ |D|\cdot\alpha & (1)\\[4pt] |C_b|\ /\ |C_{b+1}| \ \ge\ \beta & (2)\end{cases}$$

- **(1)** verifies that **most data points in the dataset are not outliers** (for instance $\alpha = 90\%$): sum cluster cardinalities in decreasing order until the sum reaches $|D|\cdot\alpha$.
- **(2)** focuses on the fact that **large and small clusters should have SIGNIFICANT DIFFERENCES in size** (for instance $\beta = 5$ means the cardinality of cluster $b$ is 5 times that of cluster $b+1$) — i.e., we look for an **abrupt variation** between adjacent sorted clusters.

**The set of large clusters is $LC = \{C_i \mid i \le b\}$ and the set of small clusters is $SC = \{C_j \mid j > b\}$.**

**The CBLOF definition:**

$$CBLOF(o) = \begin{cases}|C_i| \cdot \min\big(distance(o, C_j)\big) & o\in C_i,\ C_i\in SC \text{ and } C_j\in LC\\[6pt] |C_i| \cdot distance(o, C_i) & o\in C_i \text{ and } C_i\in LC\end{cases}$$

> **Reading the two branches:**
> - **If $o$ belongs to a SMALL cluster**: CBLOF = the cardinality of that small cluster × the **minimum distance between the object and the centroid of a LARGE cluster**. This branch targets **whether small clusters are outliers** (if so, we expect that minimum distance to be large).
> - **If $o$ belongs to a LARGE cluster**: the second formula handles the case where **one object is very far from the objects of its own cluster**.
>
> **For computing the distance between object and cluster, it is sufficient to adopt the SIMILARITY MEASURE USED IN THE CLUSTERING ALGORITHM.**
>
> **Conclusion: a HIGH value means a high probability that the object is an outlier — for either reason.**

**The FindCBLOF algorithm:**
1. **Cluster the dataset** (with its own parameters to set).
2. **Compute the value of CBLOF for each object.**

> **⚠️ A warning from the professor about parameter counts**: this technique works — **partly because it has a HIGH NUMBER OF PARAMETERS.** **In general, increasing the number of parameters means the technique probably works, but we should be wary, because SETTING all the parameters is not easy.**
>
> **The parameters in CBLOF**: $\alpha$ and $\beta$; **plus** all the parameters of the clustering algorithm (e.g., $k$ for k-means); **plus** a **threshold on CBLOF** itself.
>
> **Prof's advice: when we read papers with fantastic results, we should look at the EFFORT required to obtain those fantastic results.**

### Clustering-Based Method: Strength and Weakness

**Strength:**
- **Detects outliers without requiring any LABELED data** — we work only from similarity/dissimilarity between objects, needing no domain knowledge.
- **Works for many types of data**, since distances can be defined for different data types.
- **Clusters can be regarded as SUMMARIES of the data.**
- **Once the clusters are obtained, we need only compare any object against the clusters** to determine whether it is an outlier → **fast** *(but the clustering time still has to be paid)*.

**Weakness:**
- **Effectiveness depends HIGHLY on the clustering method used** — clustering methods **may not be optimized for outlier detection** — and also on the parameters used to determine clusters.
- **High computational cost: need to FIRST find clusters.**
- **A method to reduce the cost — fixed-width clustering:**
  - A point is assigned to a cluster if the **center of the cluster is within a pre-defined distance threshold** from the point.
  - If a point **cannot be assigned to any existing cluster, a NEW cluster is created**; the distance threshold may be learned from the training data under certain conditions.

## 6. Classification Approaches

> **The framing problem** (lecture): these methods are **very interesting but very hard to apply in reality**, because in outlier detection **the number of outliers is normally very low — we start with a heavily IMBALANCED dataset.** Learning algorithms based on **minimizing the error** make no distinction between majority and minority class, producing a **strong bias toward the majority class** — in practice, classifiers that **always answer the majority class**, because that already achieves a big error reduction.
>
> **We should therefore use rebalancing techniques (Chapter 4 §10.5), but with a very strong imbalance even these are problematic**: **oversampling** requires creating a **very large amount of synthetic data**; **undersampling** risks leaving a **very, very small training set**.

### 6.1 Method I: One-Class Model

**Idea**: train a classification model that can **distinguish "normal" data from outliers**.

**A brute-force approach**: use a training set containing samples labeled "normal" and others labeled "outlier".
- **But the training set is typically heavily BIASED**: the number of "normal" samples far exceeds the number of outlier samples.
- **Necessity to re-balance** by using undersampling or oversampling.
- **⚠️ Cannot detect UNSEEN anomalies.**

**The one-class model — the standard solution:**
> Since classification normally needs at least two classes, here we instead **build a classifier that describes ONLY the "normal" class**. **If the object is INSIDE the boundary describing the normal class, it is normal; otherwise it is an outlier.**

- **A classifier is built to describe only the NORMAL class.**
- **Learn the DECISION BOUNDARY of the normal class.**
- **Any samples that do not belong to the normal class (not within the decision boundary) are declared OUTLIERS.**

#### SVDD — Support Vector Data Description (the most popular approach)

- **Constructs a HYPER-SPHERE around the positive class data** that encompasses almost all points in the data set **with the MINIMUM radius**.
- **The SVDD classifier classifies a given test point as an outlier if it falls OUTSIDE the hyper-sphere.**
- **However, SVDD can REJECT some fraction of positively labelled data when the volume of the hyper-sphere decreases.**

> **The intuition**: start from the normal objects and build a sphere collecting all of them, built so as to have a **small volume** — we **compress it as much as possible** while still containing the normal objects. **This is the trick that solves the imbalanced-dataset problem.**

#### NN-d — Nearest Neighbour Description

A variant of the Nearest Neighbor method *(closely related to the outlierness idea of §4.3)*.

**A test object $z$ is accepted as a member of the target class provided that its LOCAL DENSITY is greater than or equal to the local density of its nearest neighbor in the training set.** The acceptance function:

$$f_{NN^{tr}}(z) = \mathbb{I}\!\left(\frac{\lVert z - NN^{tr}(z)\rVert}{\big\lVert NN^{tr}(z) - NN^{tr}\big(NN^{tr}(z)\big)\big\rVert} \le 1\right)$$

> i.e., **the distance from object $z$ to its nearest neighbor in the training set** is compared to **the distance from that nearest neighbor to ITS OWN nearest neighbor.**

### 6.2 Method II: Semi-Supervised Learning

**Situation**: we have labels, but **not many** — only a sample of the dataset is labeled.

**Semi-supervised learning: combining classification-based and clustering-based methods.**

**Method:**
- Using a **clustering-based approach**, find a **large cluster $C$** and a **small cluster $C_1$**.
- Since **some objects in $C$ carry the label "normal"**, treat **all** objects in $C$ as normal.
  > We exploit the labeled data to **LABEL the clusters** found by the clustering algorithm.
- **Use the ONE-CLASS MODEL of this cluster** to identify normal objects in outlier detection.
- Since **some objects in cluster $C_1$ carry the label "outlier"**, declare **all** objects in $C_1$ as outliers.
- **Any object that does not fall into the model for $C$ (such as an isolated point $a$) is considered an outlier as well.**

> **Why it's called semi-supervised**: we apply an **unsupervised** approach to determine the clusters, and use the **labeled data to label** the clusters — as normal or outlier.

### 6.3 Comments on classification-based methods

**Strength: outlier detection is FAST.** Once the model is built, determining whether an object is an outlier is quick.
> **Contrast**: proximity-based approaches are **not** fast, because they must compute distances between objects — computationally expensive.

**Bottleneck: quality heavily depends on the AVAILABILITY and QUALITY of the training set, but it is often difficult to obtain representative and high-quality training data.**
> **We must have at least some outliers**, otherwise these approaches are very problematic. **If we DO have labels with a sufficient number of outliers, these approaches are MORE CONVENIENT than the others**, because exploiting supervision effectively derives how the boundary between normal objects and outliers should be drawn. **In reality it is very difficult to obtain a sufficient number of outliers.**

## 7. Mining Contextual and Collective Outliers

### 7.1 Contextual Outliers I — Transform into Conventional Outlier Detection

> **The big challenge here is MODELING THE CONTEXT.** Normally we have a number of variables describing our object, but **we don't know at the beginning which describe the CONTEXT and which describe the BEHAVIOUR.** We must analyze the problem to isolate the two groups.

**If the contexts can be clearly identified, transform to conventional outlier detection:**
1. **Identify the CONTEXT of the object using the CONTEXTUAL attributes.**
2. **Calculate the outlier score for the object IN THAT CONTEXT using a conventional outlier detection method.**

*Ex. Detect outlier customers in the context of customer groups:*
- **Contextual attributes**: age group, postal code
- **Behavioral attributes**: # of transactions/year, annual total transaction amount

**Steps**: (1) locate $c$'s context, (2) compare $c$ with the **other customers in the same group**, (3) use a conventional outlier detection method.

#### Generalizing contexts when the context is too small

> **If the context contains very few customers**: for a customer $c$, if the corresponding context contains **very few or even no other customers**, evaluating whether $c$ is an outlier using the **exact** context is **unreliable or even impossible**.
>
> **To overcome this**, we can assume that **customers of similar age who live within the same area should have similar normal behavior** — i.e., we **enlarge** the context.

**The mixture-model formulation:**
- **Learn a mixture model $U$ on the CONTEXTUAL attributes**, and another **mixture model $V$ on the BEHAVIOR attributes**.
- **Learn a mapping $p(V_i \mid U_j)$**: the probability that a data object $o$ belonging to cluster $U_j$ **on the contextual attributes** is generated by cluster $V_i$ **on the behavior attributes**.
- **Outlier score:**

$$S(o) = \sum_{U_j} p(o \in U_j)\sum_{V_i} p(o\in V_i)\cdot p(V_i \mid U_j)$$

where $U$ are the contextual attributes and $V$ the behavior attributes.

### 7.2 Contextual Outliers II — Modeling Normal Behavior w.r.t. Contexts

**In some applications one CANNOT clearly partition the data into contexts.**
> *Ex.*: if a customer suddenly purchased a product unrelated to those she recently browsed, **it is unclear how many previously-browsed products should be considered as the context.**

**Instead: MODEL the "normal" behavior with respect to contexts.**
- Using a **training data set**, train a model that **predicts the expected BEHAVIOR attribute values with respect to the CONTEXTUAL attribute values**.
- **An object is a contextual outlier if its behavior attribute values SIGNIFICANTLY DEVIATE from the values predicted by the model.**

> **Using a prediction model that links contexts and behavior, these methods AVOID the explicit identification of specific contexts.**

**Methods**: a number of classification and prediction techniques can build such models — **regression, Markov Models, and Finite State Automata.**

### 7.3 Collective Outliers I — On the Set of "Structured Objects"

**Collective outlier**: objects **as a group** deviate significantly from the entire data.

**We need to examine the STRUCTURE of the data set** — the relationships between multiple data objects. **Each structure is inherent to its respective type of data:**
- **Temporal data** (time series, sequences) → explore the structures formed by time: **segments of the time series or subsequences**.
- **Spatial data** → explore **local areas**.
- **Graph and network data** → explore **subgraphs**.

> **⚠️ The difference from CONTEXTUAL outlier detection: the STRUCTURES are often NOT EXPLICITLY DEFINED, and have to be DISCOVERED as part of the outlier detection process.**

**Two categories of collective outlier detection methods.** The first:
**Reduce the problem to conventional outlier detection:**
- **Identify structure units** — treat each structure unit (e.g., subsequence, time-series segment, local area, or subgraph) **as a data object**, and **extract features**.
- **Then perform outlier detection on the set of "structured objects"** so constructed, using the extracted features.

### 7.4 Collective Outliers II — Direct Modeling of the Expected Behavior of Structure Units

The second category: **model the expected behavior of structure units DIRECTLY.**

**Ex. 1 — Detect collective outliers in an online social network of customers** *(very interesting for cybersecurity):*
- **Treat each possible SUBGRAPH of the network as a structure unit.**
- The collective outlier is an **outlier subgraph** in the social network:
  - **Small subgraphs that are of VERY LOW frequency**, or
  - **Large subgraphs that are SURPRISINGLY FREQUENT.**

**Ex. 2 — Detect collective outliers in temporal sequences:**
- **Learn a MARKOV model** from the sequences.
- **A subsequence can then be declared a collective outlier if it significantly deviates from the model.**

> **Collective outlier detection is SUBTLE**, due to the challenge of exploring the structures in data:
> - The exploration **typically uses HEURISTICS**, and thus **may be application-dependent**.
> - **The computational cost is often HIGH**, due to the sophisticated mining process.

## 8. Outlier Detection in High-Dimensional Data

> **The nightmare of data science: high dimensionality — the CURSE OF DIMENSIONALITY.** We met it in clustering (Chapter 5): when we have many dimensions, **all objects appear far from each other**.
>
> **⚠️ Why this is a problem specifically for outlier detection: if all objects appear distant from each other, the methods we've seen will classify them ALL as outliers.**

### 8.1 Challenges

- **Interpretation of outliers**
  - **Detecting outliers without saying WHY they are outliers is not very useful in high-D**, because many features are involved.
  - *E.g.*: **which subspaces manifest the outliers**, or an assessment of the "outlier-ness" of the objects.
- **Data sparsity**
  - **Data in high-D spaces are often SPARSE.**
  - **The distance between objects becomes heavily DOMINATED BY NOISE as dimensionality increases.**
- **Data subspaces** — approach the problem by working on **subspaces**:
  - **Adaptive to the subspaces signifying the outliers.**
  - **Capturing the LOCAL behavior of data.**
- **Scalability with respect to dimensionality**
  - **The number of subspaces increases EXPONENTIALLY.**

### 8.2 Approach I — Extending Conventional Outlier Detection

**Method 1: Detect outliers in the FULL space** — e.g., the **HilOut algorithm**.
- Find **distance-based outliers**, but use the **RANKS of distance** instead of the absolute distance.
- For each object $o$, find its $k$-nearest neighbors $nn_1(o),\dots,nn_k(o)$. **The weight of object $o$:**

$$w_k(o) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} dist\big(o,\ nn_i(o)\big)$$

- **All objects are ranked in WEIGHT-DESCENDING order.**
- **Top-$l$ objects in weight are output as outliers** ($l$: user-specified parameter) — they are characterized by a **very large distance to their neighbours**.

> **This is again the multivariate → univariate transformation trick**: we replace each high-dimensional object with a **single number** (its weight).

**Method 2: Dimensionality reduction.**
- **Works ONLY when, in lower dimensionality, normal instances can still be DISTINGUISHED from outliers.**
- **PCA**: heuristically, **the principal components with LOW VARIANCE are preferred**, because on such dimensions **normal objects are likely close to each other and outliers often deviate from the majority**.
  > This approach is interesting because it is **unsupervised** — applicable **without any labels**.

### 8.3 Approach II — Finding Outliers in Subspaces

**Extending conventional detection makes outlier INTERPRETATION hard. Finding outliers in much lower-dimensional SUBSPACES makes it easy to interpret WHY and TO WHAT EXTENT an object is an outlier.**

*E.g.*: find outlier customers in a certain subspace — **average transaction amount ≫ avg. AND purchase frequency ≪ avg.**

#### A grid-based subspace outlier detection method

**Project data onto various subspaces to find an area whose DENSITY IS MUCH LOWER THAN AVERAGE.** Discretize the data into a grid with **$\varphi$ EQUI-DEPTH regions**.

> **⚠️ Why EQUI-DEPTH (and not equi-width)?** Because equi-depth cells **contain the SAME NUMBER of objects** by construction — so any deviation from the expected count is meaningful. *(In illustrations the grid may look equi-width, but the widths can differ; what is constant is the object count per region.)*

**Search for regions that are significantly SPARSE.** Consider a **$k$-dimensional cube** ($k$ ranges on $k$ dimensions), with $n$ objects total.
- **If objects are INDEPENDENTLY distributed**, the **expected** number of objects falling into a $k$-dimensional region is:
$$E = \left(\frac{1}{\varphi}\right)^{k}n = f^{k}n \qquad\text{where } f = \frac{1}{\varphi}$$
- and the **standard deviation** is:
$$\sigma = \sqrt{f^{k}\big(1-f^{k}\big)\,n}$$

**The SPARSITY COEFFICIENT of cube $C$:**

$$S(C) = \frac{n(C) - f^{k}n}{\sqrt{f^{k}(1-f^{k})n}}$$

where $n(C)$ is the **actual** number of objects in $C$.

**Interpretation:**
- **If $S(C) < 0$**, $C$ contains **FEWER objects than expected** (assuming dimension independence).
- **If $S(C) > 0$**, it contains **more** objects than expected.
- **The MORE NEGATIVE, the SPARSER $C$ is — and the more likely the objects in $C$ are OUTLIERS in that subspace.**

**Algorithm:**
- **Find the $m$ grid cells (projections) with the LOWEST sparsity coefficients.**
- **Brute-force algorithm is $O(\varphi^k)$.**
- **Evolutionary algorithm** (input: $m$ and the dimensionality of the cells).

**Discussion:**
- **Very COARSE model** (all objects in a cell with fewer points than expected).
- **Quality depends on GRID RESOLUTION and GRID POSITION.**
- **Implements a GLOBAL approach** — the key criterion is the **globally** expected number of points within a cell.

### 8.4 Approach III — Modeling High-Dimensional Outliers: Angle-Based Outliers

**Develop NEW models for high-dimensional outliers directly. AVOID proximity measures and adopt new heuristics that do not deteriorate in high-dimensional data.**

**Ex. Angle-based outliers** — Kriegel, Schubert, and Zimek [KSZ08].

> **Angles are MORE STABLE than distances in high-dimensional spaces** (cf. the popularity of **cosine-based similarity** measures for text data — Chapter 2 §5.7).

**The core rule:**
- **Object $o$ is an OUTLIER if most other objects are located in SIMILAR DIRECTIONS.**
- **Object $o$ is NOT an outlier if many other objects are located in VARYING DIRECTIONS.**

> **The intuition, worked through** (lecture): take a point $a$ among the normal objects and compute the angle between the line $a\!\to\!d$ and the line $a\!\to\!e$. Considering all objects and computing many such angles, **this angle VARIES A LOT**. Now take a possible outlier $c$: **it will have almost the SAME angle with all the other objects, since it is far from all of them.**
>
> **So: the angle of a NORMAL object with other objects varies a lot; the angle of an OUTLIER with other objects varies very little.**
>
> **Equivalently: OUTLIERS are at the BORDER of the data distribution; NORMAL points are in the CENTER.**

**The model:**
- Consider, for a given point $p$, the **angle between $\vec{px}$ and $\vec{py}$** for any two $x,y$ from the database.
- Consider the **SPECTRUM of all these angles**. **The BROADNESS of this spectrum is a score for the outlierness of the point.**
- **Measure the VARIANCE of the angle spectrum**, **weighted by the corresponding distances** (for lower-dimensional data sets where angles are less reliable).

**Angle-Based Outlier Factor (ABOF):**

$$ABOF(p) = \mathrm{VAR}_{x,y\in D}\left(\frac{\langle\, \overline{px},\ \overline{py}\,\rangle}{\lVert \overline{px}\rVert^{2}\cdot\lVert \overline{py}\rVert^{2}}\right)$$

**Properties:**
- **SMALL ABOF ⇒ OUTLIER**
- **HIGH ABOF ⇒ NOT an outlier**

> **⚠️ Note the inverted convention**: unlike LOF/outlierness (where **high** = outlier), for ABOF **LOW** = outlier — because ABOF measures *variance*, and an outlier's angles barely vary.

- **⚠️ The big problem: COMPLEXITY $O(n^3)$.** Efficient approximate computation methods exist, but in general it is quite complex.
- **It can be generalized to handle arbitrary types of data.**

### 8.5 A note on modern practice: Digital Twins

> **From the lecture's closing remarks**: a classical approach used nowadays is to work with **DIGITAL TWINS**. The idea is simple: **replicate the physical system with a software system** — duplicating the behaviour of the hardware in software. Then **compare what the digital twin produces with what we measure from the physical equipment, under the same conditions.** **If we observe some variation, we probably have outliers.**
>
> This is very useful for **fault detection in industry**: generate a digital twin, compare its output with the real equipment's output to understand whether there is a deviation. **This is the FIRST step; the final goal is to PREDICT possible faults.**

## 10. Key points / potential exam pitfalls

### Foundations
- **⚠️ The noise/outlier tension is the chapter's opening paradox**: noise **must be removed before** outlier detection, but **every noise-removal technique is a SMOOTHING technique**, and smoothing **reduces sample amplitude** — which can **destroy the very outliers we want to find**. The answer is a **balance**, not a rule.
- **Outlier vs. NOVELTY**: an outlier **stays** an outlier; a novelty is an outlier **only at the beginning** and is **later merged into the model**. This is a definition-level distinction likely to be asked directly.
- **The three types, in increasing difficulty**: **global** (deviates from everything) → **contextual** (deviates *given a context*; requires splitting attributes into **contextual** vs **behavioral**) → **collective** (a *subset* deviates **even though individual members may not be outliers**).
- **⚠️ The hardest part of contextual outliers is NOT the detection** — once the context is fixed you just apply global techniques. **The hard part is DECIDING which attributes are contextual and which are behavioral**, since datasets don't come with that split.
- **Why clustering-based methods FAIL on collective outliers**: DBSCAN and friends detect **single** isolated points. **A small group of outliers looks like a legitimate small cluster**, not like noise. This is exactly why CBLOF (§5, case 3) exists.
- **One object can belong to more than one outlier type.**
- **Recall matters more than accuracy** in supervised outlier detection — missing an outlier is worse than a false alarm.

### Statistical approaches
- **⚠️ Always verify NORMALITY before applying the Gaussian methods** — via **Shapiro-Wilk** (test) or **Q-Q plot** (visual). A **straight line** on the Q-Q plot ⇒ approximately normal. Skipping this step invalidates everything downstream.
- **In the Q-Q construction, $n$ values ⇒ $n+1$ equal-area segments** (9 values → 10 regions of 10% each). Off-by-one here is a classic slip.
- **$\mu$ and $\sigma$ are NOT parameters of the method** — they are *computed* by maximum likelihood (and MLE just returns the sample mean and sample std). **The only real parameter is the THRESHOLD** (e.g. whether to use $\mu\pm3\sigma$).
- **Grubbs' test only tests the MIN or the MAX**, because in a univariate setting an outlier can only be an extreme. **Reject when $G_{test} \ge G_{critical}$** — note the direction; the worked example gives $G=2.52 \ge G_{crit}=2.23$ → the value 3 is an outlier.
- **The multivariate strategy is always the same: REDUCE TO UNIVARIATE** by replacing each object with a single number (a distance), then reuse §3.1 machinery. Mahalanobis + Grubbs is the canonical pipeline.
- **⚠️ Mahalanobis exists because Euclidean fails on two counts**: (a) **unequal weighting/units** — fixed by z-scoring; (b) **correlated dimensions** — fixed by the covariance matrix. Mahalanobis = **decorrelate → scale to unit variance → then Euclidean**. Conceptually a rotation of axes, like PCA.
- **Direction of the covariance effect**: **strong correlation ⇒ high covariance ⇒ distance REDUCED.** Its cost: **computing and storing $S$ AND its inverse**.
- **The $\chi^2$ multivariate method needs > 30 variables** for the central limit theorem to make $\chi^2$ approximately normal, and uses only an **UPPER** control limit $\overline{\chi^2}+3S_{\chi^2}$.
- **⚠️ "Non-parametric" does NOT mean parameter-free**: histograms still need a **bin size**, KDE still needs a **bandwidth $h$**. What's flexible is that the number/nature of parameters isn't fixed in advance.
- **Bin size errors go in opposite directions**: **too small → false POSITIVES** (normal objects land in empty bins); **too big → false NEGATIVES** (outliers hide in frequent bins).
- **Cost**: simple Gaussian = **linear**; mixture models/EM = several linear iterations; **KDE can be QUADRATIC**. But **once learned, per-object detection is cheap.**

### Proximity-based
- **The two DB($r$,$\pi$) formulations are equivalent**: count points within radius $r$ vs. check whether the **$k$-th nearest neighbour** (with $k=\lceil\pi\|D\|\rceil$) is farther than $r$. Be able to convert $\pi$ (a *fraction*) into an object *count* $\pi\cdot\|D\|$.
- **⚠️ DB($r$,$\pi$)'s failure on VARYING DENSITY is CONCEPTUAL, not computational.** Two global parameters cannot serve a dataset that is dense in one region and sparse in another. **No amount of tuning fixes it** — you need a *local* method (outlierness, LOF).
- **The nested-loop trick makes practice linear, not quadratic**, because for most non-outliers the inner loop **exits early**.
- **The CELL grid's $r/2$ diagonal is deliberate**: it guarantees **level-1 ⇒ $dist \le r$** and **level-2 ⇒ $dist \ge r$**. Remember the sum $r/2 + r/2 = r$.
- **⚠️ Both cell-pruning rules are ONE-DIRECTIONAL.** Failing the level-1 test does **not** prove the cell's objects are outliers — it only means they must be checked. Unpruned objects need distances computed **only against level-2 cells**, never beyond.
- **Outlierness solves varying density by making the comparison LOCAL**: object's mean kNN distance ÷ the mean of its neighbours' mean kNN distances. **> 1 ⇒ farther from neighbours than expected.**
- **⚠️ Outlierness (and LOF) still FAIL when clusters of different density are CLOSE** — the failure is at the **boundaries**. In the standard figure $p$ scores higher than $q$ and $r$ despite **not** being an outlier, because for small $k$ the dense green points aren't in $p$'s kNN list.
- **Outlierness/LOF do NOT decide anything by themselves** — they produce a **score**. You still need **$k$** AND **a threshold**. Since the scores form a univariate distribution, §3 methods can be applied to them.
- **LOF chain of definitions, in order**: $dist_k$ → $N_k$ → **reachdist** ($\max$ of the two, i.e. clamped) → **lrd** (count ÷ sum of reachdists) → **LOF** (ratio of neighbours' lrd to own lrd). Getting reachdist's $\max$ backwards breaks everything downstream.
- **$N_k(o)$ can be LARGER than $k$** when several objects tie at the same distance.
- **LOF ≈ 1 ⇒ normal; LOF ≫ 1 ⇒ outlier** — but note this reading belongs to the **normalized** form; the expanded product form used in the slides' worked example scales by $\lVert N_k\rVert^2$ while **preserving the ranking**.

### Clustering-based
- **The three cases are distinct**: (1) **no cluster** → DBSCAN gives outliers **for free**; (2) **far from its cluster's centre** → k-means needs **post-processing**, because **k-means puts EVERY object in some cluster** and therefore never outputs outliers by itself; (3) **small/sparse cluster** → CBLOF.
- **⚠️ CBLOF is the only method in this chapter that catches COLLECTIVE outliers**, because it scores **cluster size** as well as distance. Its two branches: **small cluster** → $|C_i|\times$ min distance to a **large** cluster; **large cluster** → $|C_i|\times$ distance to **its own** centre.
- **The $\alpha$/$\beta$ boundary conditions do different jobs**: **(1) $\alpha$** ensures most points aren't outliers (e.g. 90%); **(2) $\beta$** requires a **size gap** between adjacent sorted clusters (e.g. 5×).
- **⚠️ Count CBLOF's parameters**: $\alpha$, $\beta$, **all clustering parameters** (e.g. $k$), **plus a CBLOF threshold**. The professor's warning generalizes: **more parameters ⇒ better published results ⇒ harder real-world tuning.** "*Look at the effort required to obtain those fantastic results.*"

### Classification-based
- **The dataset imbalance is the whole story here.** Error-minimizing learners drift to the majority class; rebalancing helps but **breaks down under extreme imbalance** (oversampling invents too much synthetic data; undersampling shrinks the training set to nothing).
- **The ONE-CLASS MODEL is the structural workaround** — model **only the normal class** and call everything outside the boundary an outlier. **SVDD** = smallest-radius hyper-sphere around normal data; it **may reject some genuinely normal points** as the volume shrinks.
- **⚠️ The brute-force two-class approach CANNOT DETECT UNSEEN ANOMALIES** — a decisive limitation versus one-class models.
- **Semi-supervised = cluster first (unsupervised), then LABEL the clusters using the few available labels.** Objects outside the normal cluster's one-class model are also outliers.
- **Trade-off**: classification is the **FASTEST at detection time**, but **quality depends entirely on training-set availability/quality** — and getting enough labeled outliers is precisely what's hard.

### High-dimensional
- **⚠️ The curse of dimensionality bites outlier detection specifically**: when everything is far from everything, **distance-based methods flag EVERYTHING as an outlier**.
- **Three escape routes**: (I) **reduce to univariate** (HilOut weight = sum of kNN distances, rank descending, take top-$l$) or **reduce dimensions** (PCA — and note **LOW-variance components are preferred here**, the opposite of PCA's usual use in Chapter 3); (II) **search SUBSPACES** (also buys you **interpretability**); (III) **abandon distance for ANGLES**.
- **The subspace grid must be EQUI-DEPTH**, so that each cell holds the same object count and deviations are meaningful.
- **Sparsity coefficient sign convention: $S(C) < 0$ ⇒ fewer objects than expected ⇒ sparse ⇒ likely outliers.** The **more negative, the sparser**. Brute force is $O(\varphi^k)$.
- **⚠️ ABOF INVERTS the usual convention: SMALL ABOF ⇒ OUTLIER**, because ABOF is a **variance of angles**, and an outlier sees everything in roughly the same direction. Contrast with LOF/outlierness, where **large ⇒ outlier**. Cost: **$O(n^3)$**.
